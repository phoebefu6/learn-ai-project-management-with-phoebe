Part 1 · covers PMI's AI standard + adoption reporting

What AI does to the PM job 6 min live

Split your week into two piles. One pile is artifact labour - drafting the status report, reformatting the plan, chasing owners, collating four threads into one update, turning a meeting into actions. The other pile is judgment - what to escalate, what to trade away, whether that date is real, who to trust. AI collapses the first pile and cannot touch the second. Every session in this track lives on that line.

LiveThe three places AI lands first in delivery3 min▶

Across surveyed delivery teams the same three uses show up before anything else, in this order. They are not a coincidence - they are the three jobs that are pure collation, done weekly, and hated.

  • Status reporting. A week of noise compressed into something a sponsor can read. Session b6 builds this with a live scoring simulator, because a bad AI status report is worse than none - it is fluent, confident, and empty.
  • Task and owner suggestions. Turning a meeting into "who does what by when". Session b7.
  • Early-warning signals. Spotting the slip or the risk trigger before it becomes an escalation. Sessions b4 and b5.

Reporting on AI in project management puts practical experience low - roughly a fifth of project managers say they have real hands-on experience with it, about half say little or none - while adoption pressure from above is high. That gap is the opportunity this course is aimed at.

Real world

The Friday that disappeared. A delivery lead spent every Friday afternoon assembling one status report from Jira, three Slack channels and a spreadsheet. Same shape, every week, four hours. After a workspace like tonight's, the draft arrived in ninety seconds and the four hours went to the two things that actually needed her: a vendor who was quietly slipping, and an engineer who was about to resign. The report was never the job.

Self-studyWhat does NOT change - the PMI line2 min read▶

PMI published the first edition of its AI standard for portfolio, program and project management in 2026. It is deliberately technology-agnostic - it names no vendor - and human-centered. Two things in it govern this whole course:

  • Human-in-the-loop is a value source, not a safety net. The human is not there to catch the machine's mistakes; the human is where the judgment lives. Reviewing is not the point - deciding is.
  • Governance means "clear structures, roles, authority, and guardrails" inside your existing governance, with real intervention triggers and escalation paths. Not an IT side-project, and not a perfunctory review.

Translated to your desk: nothing about accountability moved. The plan is still yours. The date you commit is still yours. The report with your name on it is still yours. What moved is how long the first draft takes.

Part 2 · covers Claude Projects, context, and house rules

The workspace: one programme, one place, loaded 5 min live

A chat window with no context can only give you generic project-management advice, which you do not need. A workspace that holds your charter, plan, risk register and glossary can tell you that milestone M3 is at risk because of the vendor in risk R-07 - which is the only kind of answer worth having. The whole skill is feeding it, not phrasing prompts cleverly.

LiveWhat goes in the context pack3 min▶

Six documents. Not thirty - a bloated pack costs you attention and adds noise. These six answer almost every question a delivery week produces.

DocumentWhat it unlocks
The charter (or the mandate email, if that is all you have)Scope, priorities, non-goals - so a draft stops proposing work you already cut
The milestone plan with gate criteriaDates and definitions of done - so "on track" means something
The risk register (RAID)Named risks with triggers - so an early warning cites R-07, not "some risk"
The RACI or owner listReal names - so actions get an owner instead of "the team"
A one-page glossary of your acronymsCDC, M3, DQ, steerco - so drafts speak your programme's language
The last three or four status reportsYour house voice and the week-on-week delta
The refresh habit A context pack decays. Whatever workspace you use, replace the plan, risk register and latest report every week - five minutes, usually Monday. A stale pack produces confidently wrong answers about last month's programme, which is harder to catch than obvious nonsense.
Self-studyWhere to put it - Projects, files, connectors2 min read▶
  • A Project (Claude's persistent workspace) is the simplest home: it keeps its own instructions and files across conversations, so you stop re-explaining Northwind every Monday.
  • Skills are reusable instruction files - your house rules and your report format, written once, applied every time. Session b9 turns tonight's house rules into one.
  • Connectors (MCP) plug the workspace into Jira, Notion, Sheets and the rest, so the pack partly refreshes itself. Also session b9 - and the point where permissions stop being theoretical, which is why the leader session a2 covers it separately.
  • Availability varies by plan and changes fast. If a feature named here has moved by the time you read it, the exercise still works: the artifact is the point, the button is not.
Part 3 · the rails every later session assumes

Three rails, and why each one exists 4 min live

Techniques change every quarter. These three do not, because each one is scar tissue from a specific way AI-assisted delivery goes wrong.

LiveRail 1 - context beats prompting1 min▶

An elaborate prompt over an empty workspace produces confident generic advice. A plain question over a loaded workspace produces your programme's answer. When a draft comes back weak, the fix is almost never a cleverer instruction - it is a missing document.

LiveRail 2 - the dates come from the team1 min▶

A model asked for a plan will produce dates. They will look reasonable, be evenly spaced, and mean nothing - nobody was asked, nothing was capacity-checked. Use AI to draft the structure of a plan: the milestones, the gate criteria, the dependencies, the questions to ask. Then get the dates from the people who will be held to them, and put those in.

Real world

The plan nobody had agreed to. A PM presented an AI-drafted schedule at a kickoff. It looked immaculate - clean phases, tidy two-week increments. The engineering lead asked one question: "who told you the migration takes three weeks?" Nobody had. The plan was fiction with good typography, and the credibility cost took a month to earn back.

LiveRail 3 - a named human signs it2 min▶

Every artifact that leaves your workspace - report, escalation, risk update, plan - has one accountable human name on it, and that human read it. This is not a formality: it is exactly what PMI's standard means by human oversight with real intervention triggers rather than a perfunctory review.

  • Read before you send, especially the numbers. Fluency is not accuracy, and a plausible number is the hardest error to catch.
  • Never let a draft assert a fact you cannot source. If it says throughput improved, you should be able to point at the ticket or the metric.
  • Own the consequence. "The AI wrote it" has never once worked as an answer at a steerco, and saying it costs more credibility than the error did.
Say it once, out loud "Context beats prompting. The dates come from the team. A named human signs it." That is the spine of the whole PM track.
Demo 1 of 2

Build the Northwind workspace ★ 12 min · everyone builds

You will build this for Northwind now and rebuild it for your own programme this week. It takes about fifteen minutes once and pays back every Monday after.

Create a Project (or a dedicated folder plus a fresh conversation, if your plan has no Projects). Name it for the programme, not the tool: Northwind data platform.

Add the context pack. For Northwind tonight: the mandate email, a six-milestone plan sketch, a five-row risk register, an owner list, and a glossary of the acronyms. For your real programme: whatever exists today, even if it is thin.

Write the workspace instructions - who you are, what the programme is, what state it is in, and what you want back. Use the prompt below as the skeleton.

Test it with a question that is impossible to answer without the pack: "Which milestone is most at risk right now, and which named risk drives it?" A good workspace names M3 and R-07. A bad one gives you generic advice about risk management.

If the answer was generic, do not rewrite the question. Find the missing document, add it, ask again. That is rail one in practice.

★ Try it now - the workspace instructionsYou are supporting me on one programme. Work only from the documents in this workspace. PROGRAMME: Northwind data platform. A retailer moving from a raw operational dump to a governed warehouse with production BI. Six milestones, M1-M6. Currently week 9, M3 (raw ingest signed off) is the live gate. ME: the delivery lead. I run the weekly report, the risk register, and the steerco. HOW TO WORK WITH ME - Answer from the attached charter, plan, risk register, owner list and past reports. If something is not in them, say "not in the pack" - do not fill the gap from general knowledge. - Never invent a date, a name, a number, or a decision. Dates come from the team. - Cite the source inside the pack for any claim: the milestone id, risk id, or report week. - When I ask for an artifact, match the format of the last one in the pack. - Ask me up to three questions when the pack is genuinely missing something material.
The version that fails - no context, clever phrasingYou are an expert PMP-certified programme director with 20 years of experience. Think step by step and use best practices to produce a world-class status report for my data platform project.
Why the second one fails It has nothing to work from. Twenty imagined years of experience cannot tell it that Northwind's M3 gate is 10 August, that R-07 is a vendor SLA, or that your sponsor hates adjectives. Persona padding is not context. Every line of the first prompt is either a fact or a rule about how to handle missing facts - which is what makes it work.
Demo 2 of 2

Write your house rules - once, for every programme ★ 10 min · build your own

The workspace instructions are programme-specific. The house rules are yours: how you write, what you never allow, what a good artifact looks like. Write them tonight, carry them for years. In b9 you will turn this file into a reusable skill.

Start from the three rails, then add your own hard "never" list - the things that have burned you.

Add your artifact standards: how long a status report is, whether actions always carry owner and date, what your RAG colours actually mean.

Add your voice rules. Yours might be: short sentences, no adjectives about progress, numbers or nothing.

Paste it into your workspace instructions under a HOUSE RULES heading, and keep the file somewhere you can reuse it on the next programme.

★ The house rules starter - edit hard, then keepHOUSE RULES (apply to every artifact you draft for me) NEVER - Invent a date, a headcount, a cost, a name, or a decision. If it is not in the pack, write "TBC - ask [role]". - State a fact I cannot trace. Every claim carries a ticket id, a metric, or a report week. - Use "good progress", "on track" or "some delays" without the number behind them. - Put personal data, client-confidential terms, or anything under NDA into this workspace. ALWAYS - One accountable owner and one date per action. Never "the team". - RAG by our agreed rule, and restate the rule in the report. - Show the change since last week, not just the current state. - End anything going to a sponsor with the decision I need and by when. VOICE - Short sentences. No adjectives about progress. Numbers or nothing. - Match the format of the most recent example in the pack.
Real world

The rule that saved a steerco. One PM added a single line to her house rules: any number in a draft must carry its source. The next week the draft claimed data-quality had "improved significantly" and could not produce a ticket or a metric for it - because it had smoothed a wobble in the numbers into a trend. She caught it in ten seconds, went back to the actual dashboard, and reported the truth: quality was flat and the gate was at risk. That is the difference between a rule and a hope.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Build the workspace for a real programme of yours - not Northwind. Six documents, even if three of them are thin.
  • Ask it the impossible-without-context question: which milestone is most at risk, and which named risk drives it. If the answer is generic, find the missing document.
  • Finish your house rules file. Add at least two "never" lines that come from something that has actually gone wrong on your programmes.
  • Write down the one PM task you spend the most hours on each week. That is your target artifact for the rest of the track - and probably the status report.
  • Check what your organisation allows before you put anything real in a workspace. If nobody knows, that is a genuine finding - leader session a2 is about exactly that gap.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your draft status report comes back generic and could describe any programme. What is the fix?

Rail one: context beats prompting. Generic output is almost always a missing document, not a badly phrased question. A persona cannot know your M3 gate date.

2 · You ask for a milestone plan and get back a clean schedule with dates. What do you do with the dates?

Rail two. Nobody was asked and nothing was capacity-checked, so the dates are fiction with good typography. Draft the structure with AI, source the dates from the team who will be held to them.

3 · An AI-drafted escalation goes to the sponsor and contains a wrong figure. Who is accountable?

Rail three, and PMI's oversight requirement. Human-in-the-loop means real review with real intervention, not a rubber stamp. "The AI wrote it" has never worked at a steerco.
